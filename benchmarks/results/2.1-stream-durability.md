# Task 2.1 — stream durability and batching, measured

**Recorded 2026-08-31.** Reproduce with `python benchmarks/bench_streams.py`.

Machine: Apple M4 · 10 cores · 16 GB · macOS 24.6.0 · Docker Desktop · Redis 7.4.11 (container) · Python 3.13.

This is the record Success Criteria 2 and 3 ask for, and an input to the benchmark report in
Task 7.4. It is deliberately a decision *with* its numbers, not numbers alone.

## The question

`appendfsync always` flushes the append-only file on every write. Open Issue 003 §8.2 proposed
adopting it anyway — paired with pipelined `XADD` — on the theory that batching turns one fsync
per order into one fsync per batch, which is group commit implemented by Redis instead of by
hand. The instruction attached was: **measure it, and if the throughput is unacceptable, fall
back to `everysec` and document the one-second window explicitly rather than leaving it
implicit.**

## Results

Median of 3 runs. "Sequential" is one `XADD` awaited at a time; "StreamProducer" is the
gateway's real path, with appends in flight concurrently and drained into pipelines.

| appendfsync | Sequential | StreamProducer | Speed-up | Mean batch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `always` | 1,996 orders/sec | **72,034 orders/sec** | 36.1× | 250 records/fsync |
| `everysec` | 6,897 orders/sec | 98,051 orders/sec | 14.2× | 250 records/fsync |

`XREAD COUNT n`, per-record cost, including unpacking into contract records:

| COUNT | Per record |
|---|---|
| 1 | 159.11 µs |
| 10 | 21.90 µs |
| 100 | **8.32 µs** |

## Decision: keep `appendfsync always`

**No fallback to `everysec`. There is no durability window.**

The reasoning, in the order it actually matters:

1. **Sequential `always` really is unusable** — 1,996 orders/sec. Had the producer not batched,
   the fallback would have been forced. The theory was worth testing rather than assuming.
2. **Batched, it is 72k orders/sec.** Open Issue 003 §8.4 puts steady-state bot flow at roughly
   500 orders/sec and benchmark bursts at 20k/sec. 72k clears both with a wide margin.
3. **`everysec` buys only 36% more throughput** (98k vs 72k) in exchange for losing up to one
   second of *acknowledged* orders on power failure. Trading acknowledged orders for headroom
   the system does not need is a bad trade, and it is one the project would have to disclose.

The batching is what makes this affordable, so it is not an optimisation to remove later:
without it, criterion 2 forces the weaker setting.

## `XREAD COUNT n` batches genuinely

Per-record cost falls 19× from COUNT 1 to COUNT 100. The network round trip is the floor on
*latency* but not on *throughput*, exactly as Open Issue 003 §8.3 argued.

**One correction to the record.** §8.3 estimated "roughly 1 µs" per record at a batch of 100;
the measurement is **8.32 µs**. The estimate was for the Redis round trip alone, while this
figure includes unpacking each entry into a contract record — the work a real consumer does.
Consumers should read with `COUNT 100`; `COUNT 1` costs 19× more per record for no benefit.

## What this does not measure

End-to-end order latency through the gateway (HTTP, auth, validation) — that is Task 6.3's
load generator and Task 7.4's report. These numbers isolate the stream layer on purpose.

Nor does it measure a cold or fragmented AOF, or contention from a real consumer reading while
the producer writes. Both arrive with the ledger in Task 2.2.
